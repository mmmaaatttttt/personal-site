And yet, in spite of all of this expenditure on health and defense, life expectancy isn't the only metric that's lagging behind other countries. American happiness has decreased as well. That's according to [The World Happiness Report](https://www.worldhappiness.report/), which for years has measured a country's happiness by sampling its population and asking people to rate their current life using the [Cantril scale](https://news.gallup.com/poll/122453/understanding-gallup-uses-cantril-scale.aspx).The gist is that the scale goes from 0 to 10, where 0 is the worst possible life someone can imagine for themselves, and 10 is the best. 

If we look at a rolling three year average of how the US has scored, the trend is not good. This aggregate metric has fallen by 4% since 2014. Our absolute ranking among all nations has also fallen significantly; this is both because other countries have increased their happiness over time, and because others have not dropped as much as America.

### Happiness Regressions

Part of this may have to do with a well-known fact that billionaires would be wise to remember: money, like any other resource, has a diminishing marginal value. The more of a thing you have, in general the less valuable each additional one of those things is to you.

And when it comes to nations, this certainly seems to be true for money. Here's a chart of GDP vs that same happiness score from the table above, expanded to 127 countries where there's data from 2014 through 2025:

<Figure caption="Life Ladder Score vs GDP over time. The US, notably, scored lower in happiness in 2025 than Kosovo, despite having a GDP more than ten times larger.">
  <RegionScatterPlotContainer variant="gdp-happiness" rows={gdpHappinessByCountryYear} />
</Figure>

There are some definite trends here. In general, the higher a country's GDP, the higher the life satisfaction of its citizens. But in particular, this relationship is not linear: every dollar increase in GDP does not yield a commensurate rise in happiness. And indeed, there are many countries with significantly lower GDP and a life ladder score greater than in the United States.

A good fit for data that has this general shape is to use a logarithmic curve. This is because the logarithm also is ever-increasing, but the *rate* at which it increases slows towards 0. Using a logarithmic curve to model the decreasing marginal value of money isn't a new thing. Esteemed mathematician [Daniel Bernoulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Bernoulli) was the first to publish a paper with this kind of modeling, and he did it all the way back in [1738](https://econ.ucsb.edu/~tedb/Courses/GraduateTheoryUCSB/Bernoulli.pdf).

If we find the best fit logarithmic curve for each of our data points by year, you can see that the fit isn't half bad.

<Figure caption="Logarithmic regression curves for the data by year.">
  <UtilityCurve gdpHappinessByCountryYear={gdpHappinessByCountryYear} />
</Figure>

If you make the tangent visible, its slope is the entire point: the farther to the right you go, the smaller the slope. In other words, the higher a country's GDP, the *less impactful* more GDP will be on its life satisfaction.

What happens at the macro level happens at the micro level too. This marginal impact of money on happiness has been explored by behavioral economics, and the same shape was popularized by [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman). While economists will argue about the particulars of the shape, there's broad alignment that happiness and income have a logarithmic relationship. In fact, one of Kahneman's [last papers](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2208661120) reaffirmed this relationship among individuals within a population.

### The Cost Of Growth

The American cultural cancer celebrating the claim that "greed is good" is, in addition to being morally repugnant, not born out by any of the data. Our happiness is stagnant. Our health metrics are down. Military spending is at an all time high, and we get to pay for the privilege of waging eternal wars every time we go to the gas station.

We're also collectively destroying the planet on a quest for a mathematical impossibility: continual economic growth on a planet with finite resources. I've written before about how we can take a [mathematical lens](/stories/warming-dots) to the question of renewable resource adoption. Here, this is just another piece in the puzzle that draws us to a clear conclusion. We are pursuing a goal of unsustainable growth, for reasons that are not even clear. The windfall that many countries saw from growth in the twentieth century are now in the rearview mirror. And whatever benefits the engines of growth still provide, half a century of flawed policy has trickled them up to the top 1%.

Is there any way out of the trap we've set for ourselves? How can we jump-start some of these stagnant measures of human flourishing, while not also destroying one another or the planet?

These questions are increasingly becoming the focus of academic study. And a paper from last year, titled [Post-growth: the science of wellbeing within planetary boundaries](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2542519624003103?fr=RR-2&ref=pdf_download&rr=a4914fafbb55d8c2), does a great job of surveying current research attempts to answer some of these thorny issues.

### Growth, Green Growth, Negative Growth

One of the main focus areas of the review is a categorization of economic models into three buckets. The first is status quo: we acknowledge (or, depending on your political party, we don't) the downsides to our current social contract. But we convince ourselves that the upsides to growth outweigh the downsides, and therefore take no significant action to alter our course.

The second is a "green growth" model of the kind popularized by the Green New Deal. This approach acknowledges the ecological impact of our continued growth, and tries to correct for it by funneling our capitalistic desire for growth into transitioning that same economy into one based on renewable energy.

The third set of models is even more radical. It intentionally eschews growth. Instead of basing an economy around ensuring that GDP is forever increasing, these models optimize for the very things for which GDP has become an increasingly bad proxy. According to the authors, these include (but are not limited to) "sustainability, equity, and human wellbeing."
